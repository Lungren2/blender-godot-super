# Storytelling in games

The lesson asks how games can tell stories effectively. The procedure below adapts that question to this project's playable workflow.

## Start with the player's role

Read the core fantasy, player verbs, gameplay loop, and "Story and narrative approach" in `GAME_DESIGN.md`.

Before writing lore or dialogue, answer:

- What does the player need to know now?
- What does the player need to feel now?
- What action makes that information relevant?
- Can the player discover or cause part of the story through play?

Story should support the role the player is already performing.

## Use the strengths of an interactive medium

Prefer delivering story through play when practical:

- environment and spatial arrangement;
- consequences of player actions;
- character behavior;
- systemic interactions;
- objectives and obstacles;
- item placement;
- animation and visual state;
- optional discovery;
- dialogue that reacts to context.

Use direct exposition when it is the clearest tool, not because it is the easiest content to generate.

## Keep gameplay and narrative coherent

Check whether the story says one thing while the player's repeated actions say another.

If the game presents an urgent crisis but invites long consequence-free detours, or describes a character as weak while gameplay makes them dominant, decide whether that contrast is intentional.

Do not solve every mismatch with more dialogue.

## Give information at the moment it has value

Avoid front-loading history the player cannot yet use.

Introduce names, factions, rules, motives, and backstory when they help the player interpret a place, choice, relationship, or consequence.

Let unanswered questions create curiosity, but do not hide information the player needs to make a fair decision.

## Use environment before explanation when it is stronger

For place-based story, inspect what the player can infer from:

- damage and repair;
- abandoned objects;
- architecture;
- signs of use;
- lighting and sound;
- NPC routines;
- changes between visits;
- resource placement;
- traces of previous events.

An environment should not become a museum of lore props. Each detail should support the current place, mood, conflict, or player decision.

## Protect pacing

Narrative beats consume player attention and time.

Check whether a cutscene, dialogue exchange, text log, or environmental sequence lands at a useful point in the gameplay rhythm.

Do not interrupt mastery, high pressure, or repeated attempts with exposition the player must consume again.

## Keep lore proportional to the game

Do not expand a setting simply because more lore can be written.

Create only enough history, terminology, factions, characters, and explanation to support the experience being built.

A small game with a coherent world is better served by a few durable facts than a large unused canon.

## Test story in the playable build

Run the relevant sequence.

Observe:

- what information is visible without opening optional text;
- whether the player understands the immediate motivation;
- whether story delivery blocks control or pace;
- whether the environment supports the stated world;
- whether consequences match prior actions;
- whether optional details reward attention rather than carry mandatory information.

Use computer use for what is actually seen and heard. Use MCP for exact scene and state inspection.

## Record durable narrative rules

Update `GAME_DESIGN.md` when the project establishes a durable narrative rule, character relationship, world rule, or delivery principle.

Do not turn speculative lore into canon until the game uses it.
